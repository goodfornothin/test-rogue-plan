Ground, Breathe, Twist, Lift: The Drill Behind Real Lead-Follow Connection

16 September 2026 · An interview on the exercise we teach before any patterns at all

Before students learn a single pattern at Rogue Bachata Workshops, they practice drills: this is a two-person drill built entirely on an open hold, a weight transfer, and a twist that changes direction. The leader's palms face the ceiling, the follower's rest inside them facing the floor — no grip, nothing to hide the signal. From there, the pair travels across the studio floor and back, the direction changing every time the twist does.

QWhat made you want to isolate and teach this particular drill?
A

I've worked out a lot of this over years of experience with lead and follow, and if someone is coming into partner dance completely new, I want to give them as much of that as I can right at the outset — less trial and error, fewer injuries, fewer bad experiences with partners. Drills are how you move quickly. Concentrate on connection and balance first. Patterns can come later.

QWhat actually happens in the body right before a direction change?
A

It's never abrupt. The intention exists before the movement does — a small preparation in the opposite direction, like an inhale, as you land your previous foot. You ground into the floor, then twist — but with a lift in the vertical plane too, a small dip and rise, knees soft. Ground, breathe, twist, lift. A rounded, three-dimensional shape, not a robotic pivot.

The research

Coaches who teach torso-driven leading describe almost the same mechanism from the other side of the connection: a leader's weight shift and torso rotation register as kinesthetic cues that a follower feels through the frame, well before a turn or direction change actually lands. It's the same channel Oscar's ground-breathe-twist-lift sequence is built to use.

QHow does the follower know which direction the twist is about to go? Hand pressure?
A

As little as possible. We build connection from core to core — soul to soul. The hand is just a device, transmitting information, not creating it. When you're both going the same direction, it's a shared breath, down and up. When it's a mirror image, you isolate through the lumbar spine — bottom half twists one way, top half stays connected with your follower.

The research

This tracks with formal studies of touch-based communication in partner dance too. Research into swing dancing has found that experienced followers can decode a leader's intended movement through touch alone, even blindfolded — evidence that the hand really can function as a transmission line for something decided elsewhere in the body, rather than as the message itself.

QHow much repetition before that sensitivity clicks?
A

Differently for everyone. We run 35–40 minutes of these drills at the start of class, no goal attached, before any partner work. Weekly repetition is what does it. Now, I don't think at all when I dance — I leave it to the breathing, to the soul. We call it Rogue Listening. It's really just connection.

QWhat would you want a nervous beginner to feel reassured about?
A

Social dance isn't a competition — we say "experienced," not "advanced." Everyone is encouraged here, and the most experienced dancers are usually the best with beginners. These drills were never meant to be perfected, only attempted. Just put the effort in, and we love it.

That's the whole drill, in the end: an open hold, a weight transfer, a twist that starts in the breath before it ever reaches the feet, and a floor full of people who'd rather see you attempt it than perform it. Come cross the room with us.

Come feel a lead-follow connection built on breath and core, not hand pressure — beginners always welcome, no partner needed.
