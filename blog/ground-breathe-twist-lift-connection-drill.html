Ground, Breathe, Twist, Lift: The Drill Behind Real Lead-Follow Connection

16 September 2026 · An interview on the exercise we teach before any patterns at all

Before students learn a single pattern at Rogue Bachata, they learn this: a two-person drill built entirely on an open hold, a weight transfer, and a twist that changes direction. The leader's palms face the ceiling, the follower's rest inside them facing the floor — no grip, nothing to hide the signal. From there, the pair travels across the studio floor and back, the direction changing every time the twist does. It's deliberately generic — no bachata footwork required — which is exactly the point. We sat down with Oscar to unpack why this one exercise gets taught before almost anything else.

QWhat made you want to isolate and teach this particular drill, rather than let students pick up lead-follow instinct just by dancing?
A

I've worked out a lot of this over years of experience with lead and follow, and if someone is coming into partner dance completely new, I want to give them as much of that as I can right at the outset. That way they can start their journey with a lot less trial and error — because trial and error is where the injuries happen, and where the bad experiences with partners happen too. Whether someone is a total beginner, an improver, or even advanced, we can always drill these concepts in — it's never too late. Drills are the way to move quickly, rather than learning everything the hard way. Trial and error still has its fun, that's part of it — but I'd rather people drill this in properly than spend all their attention on patterns. Concentrate on the drills of connection and balance first. The patterns can come later.

QLet's get into the mechanics, then. What actually happens in the body, physically, right before a direction change happens?
A

A partner dance like this is about rounded motion and shape, so you can never start a twist abruptly. The intention has to exist before the movement does — it's there in your mind just before you even land your previous foot. As you're landing that foot, there's a small preparation in the opposite direction first, almost like an inhale of breath. You ground into the floor, and then you twist — but it's never just a flat, horizontal turn. There's a lift in the vertical plane too, a small dip and rise, so the knees stay soft throughout. It's ground, breathe, twist, lift — a rounded, three-dimensional shape, not a robotic pivot.

The research

Coaches who teach torso-driven leading describe almost the same mechanism from the other side of the connection: a leader's weight shift and torso rotation register as kinesthetic cues that a follower feels through the frame, well before a turn or direction change actually lands. It's the same channel Oscar's ground-breathe-twist-lift sequence is built to use.

QSo how does the follower actually know which direction that twist is about to go, in real time? Is it mostly hand pressure?
A

We try to keep it as far from hand pressure as possible — there's a little bit of it, but as little as we can manage. What we build first is a connection from core to core, or as I like to say, soul to soul. Once that's developed, you're moving from your own centre, and the other person's centre is connected to yours. The hand is just a device — it transmits information, it doesn't create it. You make the decision in your core, in your soul, and it comes out like a breath, down and up, and your partner senses that breathing before anything else happens. That's when you're both travelling in the same direction. When the direction is a mirror image — one person twisting one way, the other twisting the other way — you isolate through the lower spine, the lumbar region. Your bottom half does one twist, your top half stays connected with your follower. That's the actual drill: everyone starts moving in the same direction, then we add the mirror-image direction, and only once that's solid do we start mixing the two.

The research

This tracks with formal studies of touch-based communication in partner dance too. Research into swing dancing has found that experienced followers can decode a leader's intended movement through touch alone, even blindfolded — evidence that the hand really can function as a transmission line for something decided elsewhere in the body, rather than as the message itself.

QHow much repetition does it take before that sensitivity actually starts to click?
A

It clicks differently for everyone, and different parts of the dance click at different times for different people. What matters is doing it regularly. I don't expect anyone to practise this at home — people have busy lives — so in our current classes we run a 35 to 40 minute block of these drills right at the start, no goal attached, just doing them. Only after that do we move into partner work. If people get that weekly, the connection develops naturally rather than something they have to keep thinking about. When I dance now, I don't think at all — I leave the thinking to the breathing, to the soul. I don't plan anything. The best creativity comes without me thinking, just feeling and staying connected: being at ease with the partner, the music, the floor, everything. Listening is the real word for it. We call it Rogue Listening. It's really just connection.

QLast one — for someone who's never partner danced before and is nervous to start, what do you want them to feel reassured about, knowing this is exactly how you teach from day one?
A

Social dance isn't a competition. We actually discourage people from calling themselves "advanced" — we say "experienced" instead, because the whole essence of social dance is inclusivity. If you go to a social regularly and someone new walks in, that person is never to be disregarded — they bring fresh energy, something exciting, the same way meeting someone new at a party does. Everybody is encouraged here. And if someone doesn't want to dance with you because you're a beginner, that says more about their own understanding of the community than about you — don't let it worry you. Honestly, the most experienced dancers are usually the best with beginners, and dancing with beginners is genuinely exciting for me after all these years. So don't be afraid. We'll encourage you as long as you come with good intentions and a wish to connect through dance. And these drills were never meant to be perfected — they're meant to be attempted. Everybody finds some things easy and other things hard, and it's completely different from person to person. What I love seeing most is someone struggling with one drill and then turning around and nailing the next one — that commitment to learning is the most exciting thing there is. Just put the effort in, and we love it.

That's the whole drill, in the end: an open hold, a weight transfer, a twist that starts in the breath before it ever reaches the feet, and a floor full of people who'd rather see you attempt it than perform it. Come cross the room with us.

Come feel a lead-follow connection built on breath and core, not hand pressure — beginners always welcome, no partner needed.
