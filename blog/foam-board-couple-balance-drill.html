Breathe, Soften, Lean: The Foam Board Drill Behind Rogue Bachata's Couple Balance

18 September 2026 · An interview on the exercise that teaches partners to stop balancing alone

At Rogue Bachata Workshops, students sometimes practice the basic step and the box step with a piece of foam board wedged between them at the navel — nothing holding it there but torso and breath. Drop it, and everyone in the room knows. Keep it up, and something clicks that's harder to name: partners stop moving like two people each managing their own balance, and start moving like one.

QWhat actually happens physically when a student dances with that foam board wedged between them — what does it force their body to do differently than dancing without it?
A

The main thing we want to achieve here is to keep the simplicity in the movement. When you have a lead and follow connection, you do not want to have anything extra that brings noise to the signal between the two partners. What I see very often is that people either move their hands about, confusing their partner, or they move their hips in a way that can be confusing. Now, moving the hips is lovely when we're fully in control and we have a good connection — but before that, at the earlier stages, we should try to have stability in the hips and in the core, which is what allows this connection. This foam board in between brings that stability to the core, and you get a better core-to-core connection. How you place it — I'd suggest just below the belly, above the hips, on the bottom of the stomach, around the navel, and you get this connection there. You could place it at other heights as well, but we placed it at that level, and I did see an incredible change of movement and connection between partners, which I really appreciated.

QOnce the board's in place at that height, how do you actually cue the movement itself? When you tell them to run the basic step or box step with it there, what are you watching for to know it's working, versus when someone's fighting against it?
A

As long as they have their torso neutral, so that they don't poke their belly out — the part that's connected to the foam board shouldn't contort their body. Once that's achieved, the goal is simply to be able to move around in space without dropping that board. That's as simple as it is. Don't think about anything else, keep your body nice and relaxed — which is also quite not easy to have when you have the board there, because you tend to stop breathing. So we want to avoid that, with a relaxed body and a neutral body, to be able to move around. If you're able to move around with that sort of relaxation, and listening to your partner, you're going to be making those neuromuscular connections that will help you when you dance later.

The research

There's a real mechanical reason this drill works. Researchers modelling two people jointly holding a shared balance task have found that a mechanical coupling between two bodies actually improves stability against balancing errors compared with each person balancing alone — the shared contact, done right, makes the pair steadier than either individual would be. That's the foam board, structurally: not a test either partner can pass solo.

QThat "you tend to stop breathing" is such an honest detail — is there a specific fix you give students when you notice they've gone tense and stopped breathing, or does naming it out loud tend to be enough?
A

This is a tough one, because when you're starting something new, or doing a new challenge within something you already do, you are going to be possibly a bit tense, and not able to breathe in a very confident, very grounded way. So at first, get the breathing — and through the breathing, have a soft body. It's like a soft exterior around where you place the foam board, and if you have that soft body, then little shifts in your stomach from breathing shouldn't affect the board being wedged in the right area. If you maintain the soft exterior, you're able to accommodate slight changes in movement. First of all, you want to avoid too much difference in movement between the two partners — but you also want to keep it soft, so that if there is a movement, you're still able to maintain that connection.

QDoes the exercise change at all when it's the box step versus the basic, or when you bring in other leading drills — or is the core instruction identical regardless of which pattern they're running?
A

If you start off with a simple side basic, that's fine, you get into rhythm. But when you want to make the change and go into a box step, or any other thing, that's the true test. Because one has to, without being given any hand connection, be able to move their partner. If you want to move your partner backward, that means you're moving forward into the board — that's easier. But if you want to go backward yourself, then you have to have a very good connection. There's a term for this in a lot of physical art — in dance, or for singing, we call it appoggio in Italian. You lean into the breath, and it's a similar thing here — you lean into the connection. And if both partners are leaning into the connection, the leader can step backward, and the board has less of a chance of falling in that case.

QWhen the leader steps backward and has to lean into the connection to keep the board up, what's the follow doing differently in that moment to hold up their end of it?
A

What happens is that if the follower is leaning into the connection, and the leader just releases the lean a bit, or starts moving backward, the follower will have to follow — because they're leaning on the connection, and not just standing still with their own weight. This is what I've noticed while teaching recently: it's not about independent balance, it's about couple balance. A lead and a follow, a partnership balance, rather than each person maintaining their own. There's a couple axis, a couple centre of gravity, that you have to become aware of — and achieving that is crucial to connection. It's not one person maintaining their own balance by themselves; if they do that, they're not able to have that couple thing. You have to switch, in a partnership, to that kind of balance.

The research

This idea of a couple sharing one balance rather than two has started showing up in the lab as well as the studio. Researchers who put EEG caps on experienced tango dancers found that their brain activity begins to synchronise once they're moving well together, describing it as dancers' brains "coupling" through behaviour rather than words. Oscar's couple axis is the felt, physical version of the same phenomenon.

QIf couple balance really means one shared centre of gravity rather than two independent ones — is a couple on the dance floor, in some real sense, one body?
A

I would just say one body, you know?

QJust one body. So what changes for a student the moment they actually feel that — the moment they stop thinking "me, balancing," and start feeling like a single body?
A

From the moment there is trust that you develop with your partner — you ask somebody to dance, and from that very moment, even before you touch, a person who truly understands this will get this balance. You ask somebody who maybe isn't experienced, who doesn't understand this, and they start moving in a way that isn't a partnership way. I don't mean to say that if they're a stranger to you, they must completely embrace you or come into your space — they could not be in your space at all, and still respect this partnership. And you feel comfortable at once, rather than them not entering into that partnership. This is the balance you achieve even before you touch each other or start dancing — you get into it, and you feel this trust. You can even close your eyes — sometimes both of you can close your eyes — and you understand the spatial dynamics of the dance floor, so you don't even bump into people. At least, you may get bumped into — but you understand the dynamics of everything. And that is something very special, when you encounter it for the first time. I remember the initial phases of when I first started feeling this. And that's what gets people really hooked on social dancing.

That's the whole exercise, in the end: a piece of foam board at the navel, a torso that stays neutral, a breath that stays soft enough to bend without breaking connection — and, somewhere in the repetition, the quiet realisation that you were never really balancing alone.
